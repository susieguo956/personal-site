In Front of Me

In Chinese, the past is in front of you. The word is qian (前). The future is hou (后), behind your back. It's as if we are born starting at one hundred, and we work down to zero. When we are born, we are whole, and when we die, that is when life becomes a zero-sum game. You walk forward into what you can see and know, you walk backward into what you cannot see.

I think about this every time I think about my brother.

Calvin's smile used to arrive first. His big, bright teeth would show, then his eyes would crinkle brilliantly, then he would tug at the neck of his shirt because he was laughing too hard, and then he would gasp for air. It would reach the room, and the people in the room, before he did. Now, Calvin's smile disappears a little more each year. Each year it feels thinner, more negotiated.

There are some people you can argue back into the world. People who you can rescue with insistence. People you just need to be there for. Calvin has never been one of them, and will never be one of them. Loving him feels like using your palm to warm a glass and hoping it turns back into sand.

Calvin and Susie. These are the fateful names our parents chose from the famous daily American comic strip Calvin and Hobbes when they first moved to America. Randomly plucked, like gatherers finding fruit in the wild, but trusting it anyway because they were hungry for something.

I always wonder if our names have shaped us. If they have always been in front of us.

Susie is bright. Susie can survive a classroom. Susie can ask for directions. Susie can talk to anyone. Susie can go first.

Calvin is highly intelligent, but lacks discipline. Calvin has a vivid imagination, but has grown delusions. Calvin has a habit of getting into trouble. Calvin is a boy that should have been born assured of himself.

I liked the idea that my namesake had all these attributes. Just by virtue of me sharing a name with Comic-Susie, I could accomplish all these things. And I did. Every time I stood up to someone, she's just like Susie. Every time I said something intelligent for my age, she's just like Susie, again and again.

And every time Calvin was simply a mischievous little boy, he's just like Calvin.

Is the truth really just that I got lucky that I was named Susie? It certainly isn't the only factor, but it would be wrong to deny that, because I was Susie, I learned how to move. Because Calvin was Calvin, he learned how to absorb.

So, when I am sad, I cope by looking forward – which to me, means I look at what is directly in front of me. Well, in front, and a little down. I summon little me. The me who tried really hard to be Susie. I see her wearing her brother's clothes that are too big at the shoulders, but made her feel powerful. I see her racing to be the first to complete her times tables yet again. I see her charming the adults and receiving praise of maturity at her young age.

She is my evidence, and I look forward to her. If something fractures – a friendship, a plan, a version of myself – I imagine explaining it to her. A broken friendship is character-building, it is learning how to manage yet another form of relationship. It is the chance to try and recoup some lost beauty in an effort to produce something more beautiful. A broken plan is the chance to do something more fulfilling in its place, more correct. A broken version of myself, that's just life. I will always be broken and re-broken. I tell her that nothing in the world is inescapable, but it is always navigable if you are observant enough.

It is a very Chinese way of surviving: face the past. It has already proven that you endure.

But when I look at Calvin, I cannot find a smaller version of him to reason with. I cannot find evidence. I cannot locate an earlier brightness and project it forward. His sadness does not feel developmental; it does not feel like a phase that can be contextualized. It feels geological. And I do not know how to argue with geology.

Sometimes I wonder if my optimism is just ultra-delusioned-cope. The paragon of control. If I look at the past long enough, I can pretend the future is patterned. I can pretend I am not walking backward into something I cannot see. I can pretend I am Susie-who-has-always-figured-it-out, so I will, indeed, figure it out.

Even so, I still always try to speak to Calvin about the past. Remember when we used to go to the lake…remember how good the music you made sounded…remember when we laughed so hard at Dad…as if memory is a rope I can throw to him across a widening gap, and he just needs to grab on.

He nods. He is polite. I have not reached him.

It is possible that I love him in the wrong tense.

I love him as he was. I fear him as he might become. I struggle to sit with him as he is.

There is something really humiliating about not being able to save your own brother. I do not mean to rescue him in some dramatic sense. I mean the ordinary saving. The kind that happens through comfortable proximity, through shared language, through time spent in the same kitchen.

There is something even more humiliating about thinking, I wish my older brother would look ahead for me so I could just trust fall into his arms.

But I don't need to be caught. I am good at survival. It's my best quality, by far. I know how to narrate loss into growth. I know how to accessorize sadness so it looks intentional, meaningful. I am terrible at sitting in a room where nothing is transforming.

When I imagine little me, she is always in action. And the action is always moving forward – which is to say, she is always facing what has already been survived. She trusts that accumulation equals wisdom, and that endurance equals direction.

But when I think about Calvin, I am forced to admit something I vehemently resist: the past does not guarantee anything, it is only what is in front of you. It is not a promise. I suspect that my way of coping – looking at the past as a promise – works only because I believe in becoming. That any wound can be metabolized into insight.

What if he does not believe that? What if he is not interested in becoming?

In Chinese, you walk backward into the future. You cannot see it. You can only orient yourself by what is already known. So I keep little Susie in front of me. She is neither a solution, nor a prophecy. She is only proof that I have lived through previous versions of confusion.

Calvin walks somewhere I cannot see. I cannot drag him toward what is in front of me. I cannot rotate him toward the same orientation.

All I can do is walk beside him, backward, into whatever is behind us.

And hope that love, even without transformation, is still a form of staying.
